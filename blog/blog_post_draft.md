# When smart subsidies meet the agro-dealer shelf: Lessons from a fertilizer voucher experiment in Malawi

*Soil-test recommendations and a $100 voucher raised maize yields by a third in rural Malawi — but farmers used the voucher to buy more of the standard subsidy bundle, not the precision blend the soil tests prescribed.*

By Thomas Assefa, Jonathan Atkinson, Joachim De Weerdt, Edwin Siyame, David J. Spielman, Hailemariam Ayalew, and Bjorn Van Campenhout | May 13, 2026

---

### Key takeaways

- Soil-test recommendations delivered on their own produced no measurable change in fertilizer use, nutrient application, or maize yields.
- The same recommendation paired with a roughly $100 voucher raised fertilizer use on the targeted plot and lifted maize yields by about a third.
- Voucher recipients spent the transfer on the standard urea-and-NPK bundle, not on the lime, potassium products, CAN, or MAP that the soil tests prescribed.
- Agro-dealer product depth — what is on the shelf, in what package sizes, at what prices — is identified as a binding constraint on precision fertilization.

---

For more than a decade, "smart subsidies" have been the reform proposal of choice for Africa's input subsidy programmes. The logic is appealing on paper: replace a one-size-fits-all bundle of fertilizer, delivered through a parastatal channel, with a flexible voucher that farmers redeem at commercial agro-dealers for products matched to their soil. Doing so, the argument runs, should raise agronomic precision, deepen private input markets, and reduce the political capture and fiscal cost that have dogged universal programmes.

Malawi's Affordable Input Programme (AIP) — a 100-kg bundle of NPK basal blend and urea topdressing distributed each season to a rationed share of households — is exactly the kind of programme this reform agenda has in mind. We set out to test what would happen if a piece of the AIP budget were redirected through a smart-subsidy channel. The answer, from a year-long randomized trial across 113 villages in central Malawi, is more bounded than the policy briefs anticipated.

### Experimental design

Working with the agronomy team at Meridian and the Farm Service Unit, we randomized villages into three groups. In the first, a trained agronomist visited a farmer-selected plot, took soil samples, and returned with a tailored fertilizer recommendation. In the second, farmers received the same soil-test recommendation plus a voucher worth 170,000 Malawi kwacha — roughly $100, deliberately benchmarked against the implicit value of the AIP bundle — redeemable for fertilizer at a named chain of Farmer World agro-dealers. A third group received neither and served as a comparison. At the end of the season we measured fertilizer use, maize yields, and AIP receipt, and collected data on two plots per household: the soil-tested plot and a second, randomly selected plot.

### The recommendation alone

The recommendation-only arm produced no measurable change in fertilizer purchased, nutrients applied, maize harvested, or the likelihood of receiving an AIP bundle. The recommendation was delivered at high rates, comprehension was high, and farmers in this arm reported intending to follow the advice. Realised input decisions did not respond.

This null is consistent with the broader literature on information-only interventions in smallholder agriculture, but it is informative here precisely because the advice came from a credible, well-resourced commercial agronomy service rather than a stretched public extension system. If credentialed advice on a farmer's own plot does not move behaviour, blanket extension messaging and soil-health-card schemes are unlikely to do so either.

### What the voucher actually bought

The voucher arm tells a different and more nuanced story.

On the soil-tested plot, voucher recipients applied roughly 30 kg more fertilizer than the comparison group and harvested about a third more maize. The productivity gain is real, and it is identified off the voucher: the information arm by itself produced no such gain.

Decomposing the voucher purchases by product, however, qualifies the precision-fertilization interpretation. The 30 kg increment is split almost evenly between urea (about 18 kg) and NPK 23:10:5 basal blend (about 19 kg). That 50/50 split is, to a close approximation, the composition of the AIP bundle. Where the soil tests recommended something different from the modal Malawi maize prescription, farmers did not buy it:

- Potassium products (MOP, SOP, or potassium sulphate) were prescribed for around 69% of households in our sample. Application in the voucher arm was essentially zero, and lower than in the comparison group.
- Lime was recommended for about a quarter of households, at roughly 950 kg per hectare. Not a single household in any arm applied lime.
- Calcium ammonium nitrate (CAN) was the recommended topdress for the modal recommendation; voucher recipients used less CAN than the recommendation-only arm.
- MAP was prescribed for a smaller share of farmers; uptake was zero in every arm.

The voucher pulled farmers toward the standard Malawi maize bundle and away from the distinctive components of their own soil-test recommendation. The productivity gain came from intensification of fertilizer products farmers were already using, not from adoption of the precision blend the soil tests prescribed.

This contrasts with the most-cited prior study on the same question. A Tanzanian experiment by Harou and co-authors (2022) found that pairing a voucher with a soil-test recommendation directed purchases toward the recommended product, while a voucher alone expanded generic fertilizer purchases. In our Malawian setting, the recommendation-plus-voucher arm behaved closer to what Harou and co-authors attribute to voucher-only behaviour. The result is not that farmers ignored the soil test, but that the products needed to act on it did not flow through the channel where the voucher was redeemable.

### Effects on AIP receipt

A natural concern with any commercial-channel reform is that it will simply substitute for the existing subsidy and leave fiscal cost unchanged. Voucher households in our sample were eight percentage points less likely to receive AIP — a notable drop, given that 19% of comparison households received it — and household-level AIP fertilizer fell by about 8.5 kg. But 8.5 kg is roughly one-tenth of the voucher's face value. The two programmes mostly stacked. Combined with the product-mix finding, this means voucher households ended up with substantially more fertilizer than comparison households, but of essentially the same composition the comparison household would have received from AIP. Any future voucher-based reform will need to be integrated with AIP rather than run alongside it, or fiscal costs will compound.

### The agro-dealer supply constraint

Our experiment paired information (a soil test from a credentialed agronomist on the farmer's own plot) with finance (a voucher equivalent to the AIP bundle's value, redeemable at a named chain of commercial agro-dealers). Neither component, nor the combination, was sufficient to deliver the recommended product mix. What this points to is the supply chain — which products agro-dealers actually stock, in which package sizes, at what prices, and with what familiarity to farmers asking for them at the counter. Lime in 950-kg quantities, potassium sulphate, MAP: these are not products that move briskly through rural Malawian agro-dealer networks, and the experiment suggests that even a well-funded, well-informed farmer arriving at the till will default to the urea-and-NPK bundle that the system already knows how to deliver.

This conclusion requires careful framing. The experiment identifies agro-dealer product depth as **a** binding constraint on precision fertilization, not necessarily the only one. Information and farmer-side constraints — comprehension, trust in unfamiliar products, the operational complexity of applying lime or sulphur-containing blends — could still bind alongside supply. What the data establish is that information and finance, even when paired, are not sufficient. Closing the gap requires moving on margins beyond those two.

### Implications for input-subsidy reform

The case for routing subsidies through commercial channels rests on more than agronomic precision: targeting, fiscal discipline, and private market development are all part of the argument, and our experiment was not designed to measure those. The precision-fertilization rationale, however, is not supported by the data under current Malawi agro-dealer conditions. Smart subsidies as currently deployable deliver intensification of the existing bundle through a commercial channel, rather than a soil-matched product mix.

The actionable lever this points to is the agro-dealer network itself. Investments in product depth, smaller package sizes for lime and specialty fertilizers, agro-dealer training, and reliable stocking of the products that soil tests actually recommend would change what a voucher can buy. Without those upstream investments, layering vouchers and soil tests on top of AIP is unlikely to deliver the precision agriculture that the policy debate envisions. With them, the smart-subsidy approach has a chance of delivering what it promises.
