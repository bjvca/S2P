# Vouchers, soil tests, and the limits of smart fertilizer subsidies: Evidence from a Malawi experiment

*Fertilizer recommendations based on a soil test, paired with a $100 voucher, raised maize yields by a third in rural Malawi — but farmers used the voucher to buy more of the standard subsidy bundle, not the precision blend the recommendation prescribed.*

By Thomas Assefa, Jonathan Atkinson, Joachim De Weerdt, Edwin Siyame, David J. Spielman, Hailemariam Ayalew, and Bjorn Van Campenhout | May 13, 2026

---

### Key takeaways

- Fertilizer recommendations based on a soil test, delivered on their own, produced no measurable change in fertilizer use, nutrient application, or maize yields.
- The same recommendation paired with a roughly $100 voucher raised fertilizer use on the targeted plot and lifted maize yields by about a third.
- Voucher recipients spent the transfer on the standard urea-and-NPK bundle, not on the lime, potassium products, CAN, or MAP that the soil-test-based recommendation prescribed.
- The experiment cannot identify why farmers ignored the precision blend, but the pattern is consistent with supply-side factors — limited agro-dealer stocking of less-common products, package size, price — binding alongside any farmer-side constraints.
- More broadly, upgrading one or two links of an agricultural value chain — soil tests, AI-assisted extension, digital tools, satellite diagnostics — does not deliver precision outcomes when the remaining links stay generic.

---

Africa's input subsidy programmes have been the subject of a long-running reform debate. One prominent strand of that debate, the "smart subsidy" approach, would replace one-size-fits-all bundles delivered through parastatal channels with flexible vouchers redeemable at commercial agro-dealers. The agronomic case rests on the precision-agriculture promise: rather than every household receiving an identical fertilizer package regardless of what their soil actually needs, each household would receive a recommendation tailored to a soil test on their own plot, with the voucher used to buy the products and quantities that test prescribes. The argument runs that this should deepen private input markets and reduce the political capture and fiscal cost that have dogged universal programmes.

Malawi's Affordable Input Programme (AIP) — a 100-kg bundle of NPK basal blend and urea topdressing distributed each season to a rationed share of households — is the kind of programme this reform debate centres on. We set out to test what would happen if a piece of the AIP budget were redirected through a smart-subsidy channel. Results from a year-long randomized trial across 113 villages in central Malawi suggest that the reform delivers higher fertilizer use and higher yields, but not the precision fertilization that motivates the smart-subsidy case.

### Experimental design

Working with the agronomy team at Meridian and the Farm Service Unit, we randomized villages into three groups. In the first, a trained agronomist sampled soil on a farmer-selected plot and returned with a fertilizer recommendation tailored to that plot's measured nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, sulphur, and micronutrient profile — a hyper-local, individually-customised prescription specifying specific fertilizer products or blends, application rates, and quantities. In the second group, farmers received the same soil test based recommendation plus a voucher worth 170,000 Malawi kwacha — roughly $100, deliberately benchmarked against the implicit value of the AIP bundle — redeemable for fertilizer at a named chain of Farmer World agro-dealers. A third group received neither and served as a comparison. At the end of the season we measured fertilizer use, maize yields, and AIP receipt, and collected data on two plots per household: the soil-tested plot and a second, randomly selected plot.

### The recommendation alone

The recommendation-only arm produced no measurable change in fertilizer purchased, nutrients applied, maize harvested, or the likelihood of receiving an AIP bundle. This null is consistent with the broader literature on information-only interventions in smallholder agriculture, but it is informative here precisely because the advice came from a credible, well-resourced commercial agronomy service rather than a stretched public extension system. If credentialed advice on a farmer's own plot does not move behaviour, blanket extension messaging and soil-health-card schemes are unlikely to do so either.

### What the voucher actually bought

The voucher arm tells a different and more nuanced story.

On the soil-tested plot, voucher recipients applied roughly 30 kg more fertilizer than the comparison group and harvested about a third more maize. The productivity gain is real, and it is identified off the voucher: the information arm by itself produced no such gain.

Decomposing the voucher purchases by product, however, qualifies the precision-fertilization interpretation. The 30 kg increment is split almost evenly between urea (about 18 kg) and NPK 23:10:5 basal blend (about 19 kg). That 50/50 split is, to a close approximation, the composition of the AIP bundle. Where the soil-test-based recommendation prescribed something different from the modal Malawi maize prescription, farmers did not buy it:

- Potassium products (MOP, SOP, or potassium sulphate) were prescribed for around 70% of households in our sample. Application in the voucher arm was essentially zero, and lower than in the comparison group.
- Lime was recommended for about a quarter of households, at roughly 950 kg per hectare. Not a single household in any arm applied lime.
- Calcium ammonium nitrate (CAN) was the recommended topdress for the modal recommendation; voucher recipients actually used less CAN than the recommendation-only arm.
- MAP was prescribed for a smaller share of farmers; uptake was zero in every arm.

The voucher pulled farmers toward the standard Malawi maize bundle and away from the distinctive components of their own fertilizer recommendation. The productivity gain came from intensification of fertilizer products farmers were already using, not from adoption of the precision blend the recommendation prescribed.

This contrasts with the most-cited prior study on the same question. A Tanzanian experiment by Harou and co-authors (2022) — the rare design with both a voucher-only and a recommendation-plus-voucher arm — found that the bundle shifted half of farmers toward the soil-test-prescribed product (ammonium sulfate, the relevant input where soils were sulfur-deficient), compared with only 4% of farmers in the voucher-only arm. Our Malawian voucher arm fails to deliver the equivalent product-mix shift.

### Effects on AIP receipt

A natural concern with any commercial-channel reform is that it will simply substitute for the existing subsidy and leave fiscal cost unchanged. Voucher households in our sample were eight percentage points less likely to receive AIP — a notable drop, given that 19% of comparison households received it — and household-level AIP fertilizer fell by about 8.5 kg. But 8.5 kg is roughly one-tenth of the voucher's face value. The two programmes mostly stacked. Combined with the product-mix finding, this means voucher households ended up with substantially more fertilizer than comparison households, but of essentially the same composition the comparison household would have received from AIP. Any future voucher-based reform will need to be integrated with AIP rather than run alongside it, or fiscal costs will compound.

### Why doesn't the voucher deliver precision?

Our experiment paired information (a soil test from a credentialed agronomist on the farmer's own plot) with finance (a voucher equivalent to the AIP bundle's value, redeemable at a named chain of commercial agro-dealers). Neither component, nor the combination, was sufficient to deliver the recommended product mix. The pattern — zero application of lime, MAP, and potassium products in every arm, with the voucher buying urea and NPK 23:10:5 in close to AIP proportions — suggests that the input supply chain is not adapted to the products that hyper-local soil-test recommendations prescribe. Stocking, package sizes, prices, and agro-dealer familiarity with less-common inputs all plausibly contribute.

### Implications for input-subsidy reform

The case for routing subsidies through commercial channels has several components — targeting, fiscal discipline, private market development, and agronomic precision. On the agronomic-precision claim, our data are clear: under current Malawi agro-dealer conditions, the smart-subsidy approach delivers intensification of the existing bundle through a commercial channel rather than a soil-matched product mix.

If supply-side stocking is in fact a binding constraint, the actionable lever is the input-supply side itself. Investments to broaden the range of products agro-dealers stock, introduce smaller package sizes for lime and specialty fertilizers, train agro-dealers on the recommended products, and ensure reliable availability of the products that soil tests actually recommend would change what a voucher can buy. Without those upstream investments, layering vouchers and soil tests on top of AIP is unlikely to deliver the precision agriculture that the policy debate envisions.

The point generalises beyond fertilizer subsidies. A wave of recent agricultural-development investment is directed at one or two visible links of the value chain: AI-assisted extension, digital decision-support tools, satellite-derived plot diagnostics, precision-irrigation advice, hyper-local weather and pest alerts delivered to farmers' phones. The implicit assumption is that upgrading these front-end advisory components is the binding constraint on adoption. Our experiment in Malawi suggests that assumption deserves scrutiny. An agricultural value chain has many links: soil diagnostics, advisory delivery, product portfolios at the dealer, package sizes, prices, working capital for stockholding, transport, application equipment, and the credit and risk tools that determine what a farmer can actually buy and apply. Upgrading one or two of those links to a high-tech standard while leaving the others generic does not deliver high-tech outcomes — the chain runs at the speed of its slowest link. The components of any agricultural-technology system need to be upgraded together. Precision advice without precision supply delivers neither.
