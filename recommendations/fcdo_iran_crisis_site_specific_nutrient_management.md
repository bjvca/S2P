# Short Input On Site-Specific Nutrient Management And The Iran Fertilizer Crisis

## Draft Response To Ruth

Ruth -- quick reflections from the soil-testing side. I agree that the policy case for using soil and geospatial information is stronger than it was five years ago: the tools are better, data infrastructure has improved, and delivery channels through digital advisory systems are more developed. But the evidence on whether site-specific nutrient recommendations by themselves change farmer behavior and yields is still mixed. The ``pipe dream'' critique remains relevant: more precise agronomic recommendations do not automatically solve the practical constraints farmers face when trying to act on them. Our Malawi results reinforce that caution.

In our 113-village experiment, the recommendation-only arm was delivered successfully but generated little evidence of changes in fertilizer use, nutrient application, maize yield, or economic returns. The stronger effects came when the same recommendation was paired with a voucher tied to the recommended fertilizer bundle. That combination increased follow-through, fertilizer and nutrient use, and maize yield. But compliance was partial: farmers responded mainly through familiar and available products such as NPK 23:10:5 and urea, while exact adoption of the full recommended bundle remained very limited.

The implication is that soil and geospatial tools can help allocate scarce nitrogen more efficiently, but not as stand-alone information products. In the short run, the realistic use is probably targeting and adaptation of existing fertilizer programs: use soil maps, crop-response models, and advisory platforms to identify high-return geographies/crops and adjust recommendations where nitrogen is scarce. But this has to ride on subsidy and supply-chain architecture that can actually deliver the relevant products. The binding constraint is not only recommendation quality; it is whether farmers can finance, find, and implement the recommendation.

So the short version for FCDO is: site-specific nutrient management can help make scarce-N allocation more efficient, but crisis-scale impact depends on embedding these tools in fertilizer subsidy, agro-dealer, and product-supply systems rather than treating advisory alone as the solution.

## Caveats Before Sending

- Do not call the voucher generic. In our study it was tied to the recommended fertilizer bundle.
- Do not say that farmers adjusted timing more than product mix unless we reintroduce and verify the timing/method outcomes. We previously decided those measures were not clean enough for the main paper.
- Do not use the claim that about 70 percent of the fertilizer effect was offset by AIP displacement unless that exact calculation is checked against the current AIP table.
- Safer phrasing for the recommendation-only arm is: "generated little evidence of changes" rather than "is a null."
- Avoid saying that evidence now clearly shows tailored recommendations work. The evidence base is broader than five years ago, but still mixed; the defensible claim is about better tools and better potential for targeting scarce fertilizer, not advisory-only impact at scale.
