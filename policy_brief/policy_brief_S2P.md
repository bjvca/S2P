# Same Budget, Smarter Spend: Making Malawi's Fertilizer Subsidy Work Harder

**A policy brief for the Ministry of Agriculture and the Ministry of Finance, Republic of Malawi**

*Based on a randomized controlled trial (RCT) across 113 villages in central Malawi (Kasungu, Dowa, Ntchisi, and Mchinji), testing whether soil tests and fertilizer vouchers can raise the return on the country's largest agricultural expenditure.*

---

## The bottom line

Malawi does not need to spend more on fertilizer to get more from it. In a controlled trial, giving farmers a soil-test recommendation on its own changed nothing. Pairing that recommendation with a flexible voucher of the same value as the Affordable Inputs Programme (AIP) bundle raised maize yields by about 19 percent. But farmers used the flexible voucher to buy the **same** urea and nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium (NPK) products the AIP already hands out, not the site-specific blends their soil tests prescribed.

The policy reading is direct and, for once, cheap to act on:

- **Information is not the binding constraint.** Soil tests alone do not move behaviour.
- **A flexible voucher delivers the AIP's yield gains at comparable cost, through private agro-dealers.** That is a delivery-efficiency opportunity, not a precision-agriculture one.
- **"Smart" precision fertilization will not happen until the products exist on the shelf.** Paying for soil tests before that is paying for advice no one can act on.

This brief lays out a phased reform that keeps everything farmers and politicians like about the AIP (support that reaches farmers, higher yields) while cutting waste, building private input markets, and preparing the ground for genuine precision fertilization, **at no additional fiscal cost**.

---

## Why this matters now

Fertilizer subsidies absorb a large share of Malawi's agricultural budget, and global fertilizer prices remain volatile after successive shocks to Black Sea trade and Gulf energy shipping. Every kwacha spent subsidising the wrong nutrient, or subsidising fertilizer a household would have bought anyway, is a kwacha not spent on roads, irrigation, or extension. With fiscal space tight, the question has shifted from *whether* to subsidise fertilizer to *how to deliver the same support for less waste and more output*. This trial was designed to answer exactly that.

## What we did

We worked with an agronomy team to randomly assign 113 villages to three groups:

1. **Recommendation only.** A trained agronomist took a soil sample from a farmer-chosen plot and delivered a customised fertilizer prescription (products, blends, and rates matched to that plot's nutrients).
2. **Recommendation plus voucher.** The same prescription, plus a voucher worth 170,000 Malawi kwacha (MWK), about US$100, benchmarked to the value of the AIP bundle and redeemable for **any** fertilizer at a named commercial agro-dealer.
3. **Comparison group.** Neither.

Because assignment was random, differences in outcomes are caused by the programme, not by which farmers happened to receive it.

## What we found

**1. A soil-test recommendation on its own does nothing.** No measurable change in fertilizer use, nutrient application, maize yield, or subsidy participation. In a setting where the national subsidy has anchored expectations around one standard bundle for two decades, better information is not what is missing.

**2. Money works, but farmers buy the familiar bundle.** The voucher raised fertilizer use on the tested plot by about 30 kg and maize yield by about 19 percent (roughly 151 kg per household). But the extra fertilizer was overwhelmingly urea (about 18 kg) and NPK 23:10:5 (about 19 kg), a near-perfect copy of the AIP bundle.

**3. The precision products were prescribed but never bought.** Potassium products were recommended for roughly two-thirds of households and lime for about a quarter. Uptake of both was essentially zero, in every group. Calcium ammonium nitrate (CAN) and monoammonium phosphate (MAP), also recommended, saw no uptake. The likely reasons are practical, not informational: these products are not reliably stocked, come in package sizes too large for a single plot, and are unfamiliar.

**4. The voucher mostly displaced spending farmers would have made anyway.** Much of the voucher fertilizer substituted for fertilizer households would have bought commercially or received through the AIP. That makes the voucher very attractive **to the household** (it raised farmer profit by about 190,000 MWK, half of which is money saved on purchases they no longer had to make) but limits its **additional** contribution to national production. This is the classic crowding-out result, and it is central to reading the cost-benefit numbers below.

---

## The numbers: a cost-benefit snapshot

The programme's return to the public purse depends almost entirely on **how maize is valued**. Most beneficiary households are net maize buyers (around 44 percent of maize consumption in Malawi is met through the market), so the output they gain is worth far more at lean-season retail prices than at harvest-time farmgate prices.

**Per-beneficiary social cost-benefit of the recommendation-plus-voucher package** (yield gain of 151 kg valued at different maize prices; voucher cost of 170,000 MWK):

| Valuation scenario | Maize price (MWK/kg) | Value of extra output (MWK) | Voucher cost (MWK) | Benefit-cost ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Harvest-time farmgate (sample median) | 750 | 113,000 | 170,000 | **0.67** |
| Break-even | 1,126 | 170,000 | 170,000 | **1.00** |
| Lean-season retail (2025) | 1,360 | 205,000 | 170,000 | **1.21** |
| Peak retail (prior year) | 1,742 | 263,000 | 170,000 | **1.55** |

*Read this way:* valued at the price farmers receive when they sell just after harvest, the voucher does **not** pay for itself. Valued at the price the same households pay when they buy maize back in the lean season, it clears its cost comfortably. The single most important lever on the programme's value is therefore **timing and price**, not agronomy.

**Two cautions that make this an honest brief, not a sales pitch:**

- **The soil test is an added cost that bought no behaviour change.** The table above counts only the voucher. Add the per-farmer cost of the soil test and the ratios fall further. On current evidence, the diagnostic is pure overhead until the products it recommends can be purchased.
- **Household gain is not the same as national gain.** The 190,000 MWK profit gain to the household includes about 94,000 MWK of spending the voucher displaced. That is a real benefit to the family, but from the Treasury's standpoint it is a transfer, not new production. The reform case rests on delivering support **more efficiently**, not on the headline household profit figure.

**A fiscal-reallocation illustration.** In our study area, fewer than one in five households received AIP fertilizer in a given season, a consequence of rationing. A voucher of AIP-equivalent value reaches a household at the same per-beneficiary cost, but flows through commercial agro-dealers rather than a parallel public distribution chain. If commercial delivery reduces leakage and handling costs even modestly (a channel this trial did not price but which the wider evidence base repeatedly documents), the **same budget** either reaches more farmers or puts more fertilizer in intended hands. That is the prize: not a bigger bill, a better-spent one.

---

## Five recommendations

Each follows directly from a specific result above.

**1. Do not pay for soil tests until the products they prescribe can be bought.**
*Why:* The recommendation-only group changed nothing, and even with money in hand farmers did not buy the prescribed precision products. A soil test that cannot be acted on is a cost with no return. Sequence diagnostics **after** the supply chain, not before.

**2. Fix the shelf before promoting precision.**
*Why:* Potassium, lime, CAN, and MAP were prescribed but essentially never purchased, most plausibly because they are not stocked, come in oversized packs, and are unfamiliar. Invest first in agro-dealer stocking of precision blends, smaller pack sizes suited to a single plot, and on-farm demonstration. Precision fertilization is a **supply-and-familiarity** problem, not an information problem.

**3. Pilot flexible commercial vouchers as a delivery channel for the AIP, and measure leakage head-to-head.**
*Why:* At comparable cost, the voucher delivered the same yield gains as the in-kind bundle, but through the private sector. The case for switching is targeting, reduced diversion, and market development, not agronomy. Run a costed pilot that compares leakage and administrative cost of vouchers against in-kind distribution directly, and let that number decide the scale-up.

**4. Value and time the subsidy to the consumption calendar.**
*Why:* The programme's return more than doubles when output is valued at lean-season retail prices, which is what most beneficiaries, as net buyers, actually face. Pair input support with grain storage, warehouse receipts, or delivery timing that lets households capture the high-value window instead of selling low at harvest.

**5. Change the scorecard: budget for net farmer profit and additional production, not kilograms distributed.**
*Why:* The voucher's headline yield gain masks that much of the household benefit came from displacing purchases farmers would have made anyway. Track crowd-out-adjusted additionality and net profit so the budget is judged on **new** production it creates, not on inputs it would have been bought regardless.

---

## The opportunity

Malawi can make its single largest agricultural expenditure work harder without spending a kwacha more. The evidence supports a simple, politically safe sequence:

1. **Shift AIP delivery toward flexible commercial vouchers** to capture targeting and market-building gains at equal cost. Farmers still receive support; yields still rise; the private input market grows.
2. **Reinvest the efficiency savings in the agro-dealer supply chain and farmer familiarity** that precision fertilization actually requires.
3. **Layer soil-based diagnostics on top only once the recommended products can be bought**, so the country pays for precision advice when, and only when, farmers can act on it.

This turns a static, in-kind handout into a market-building, precision-ready system. It preserves everything voters value about the subsidy and removes what the Treasury cannot afford: waste. The reform costs nothing extra to begin; it costs something only if we keep paying for advice no farmer can follow.

---

*Methods and caveats: Findings are from a pre-registered cluster-randomized trial of roughly 2,000 households; effects are intention-to-treat and reflect one agricultural season in central Malawi. The trial delivered the voucher on top of the existing AIP, so it identifies the effect of adding a recommendation-paired voucher within the current subsidy regime; the "voucher as a full replacement for the AIP" comparison is an extrapolation supported by, but not directly measured in, the design. Price scenarios use observed sample and market prices. The soil-test unit cost is excluded from the ratios above and should be added for any programme-level accounting.*
